# Hajime Japan Pricing Strategy

## Pricing objective

Hajime prices the value of trusted coordination, not the number of emails sent or boxes checked. The fee must fund preparation, responsive human service, qualified partner management, quality control, and intervention when a launch deviates from plan.

Pricing should be simple enough to understand, high enough to support excellent delivery, and explicit about professional fees and external costs.

## Why premium pricing works

### The client is buying scarce attention

The named coordinator understands the whole project, prepares handoffs, and notices when dependencies drift. That judgment and continuity are the core service.

### The avoided cost is meaningful

The direct consequences of poor coordination include founder distraction, provider rework, missed windows, duplicate translations, unsuitable office commitments, delayed banking readiness, and an incorporated company that cannot operate cleanly.

### Quality requires operating slack

A concierge business cannot run every coordinator at maximum theoretical capacity. Premium pricing supports room for escalation, proactive preparation, and unexpected complexity.

### Curated partners require investment

Hajime must vet, onboard, measure, and maintain alternatives in each critical category. A cheap coordination fee cannot support resilient partner coverage.

### The emotional outcome has value

Clients pay to feel informed and in control without personally carrying every detail. Calm, honest clarity is not an incidental benefit; it is part of the product.

## Pricing architecture

### Fixed package fee

Use a fixed project fee when assumptions, included services, client count, entity count, and target route are sufficiently standard. Fixed pricing creates confidence and rewards operating efficiency.

The proposal states:

- Included Hajime services.
- Key assumptions and client responsibilities.
- Included number of entities, founders, locations, and partner paths.
- Service window and response standard.
- External and professional fees included or excluded.
- Conditions that trigger a scope review.

### Scoped add-on fee

Use outcome-based add-ons for known additional work such as a second banking path, expanded translation coordination, another shareholder, or licensing coordination.

Avoid billing every minor variation. Add-ons should represent a real change in effort, expertise, risk, or partner coordination.

### Recurring retainer

Post-launch and annual coordination should use monthly or annual retainers tied to a defined request scope, response standard, review cadence, and number of entities. Material projects remain separately scoped.

### Enterprise fee

Enterprise pricing can combine program design, dedicated capacity, per-launch fees, country readiness, reporting, and recurring governance. Price reflects coordination complexity, volume commitment, service levels, procurement burden, and partner-capacity reservation.

### Pass-through and partner fees

Government fees, rent, deposits, insurance premiums, bank charges, translation, notarization, and professional services are shown separately unless expressly bundled.

If Hajime marks up, receives a referral fee, or benefits economically from a partner selection, the arrangement must be lawful, documented, and disclosed as required. Fit and quality remain the selection standard.

## Value communication

Price should be explained through the operating burden Hajime removes:

- One launch plan instead of disconnected provider checklists.
- One coordinator instead of the founder chasing every party.
- Prepared partner meetings and consolidated evidence requests.
- Earlier detection of dependencies and delay.
- Qualified provider selection and backup coverage.
- Clear status, ownership, and next action.
- Operational readiness after formation.

Do not justify premium pricing with vague prestige, artificial scarcity, or claims of guaranteed speed. Show the work Hajime owns, the decisions it prepares, and the risks it reduces.

## Pricing inputs

Package placement and add-ons consider:

- Number of entities and countries.
- Founders, shareholders, beneficial owners, and decision-makers.
- Ownership and source-of-funds complexity.
- Business activity and regulatory sensitivity.
- Language, translation, certification, and physical-document needs.
- Address, immigration, banking, tax, payroll, and licensing dependencies.
- Target timing and availability of responsible parties.
- Number and type of partner assignments.
- Reporting, procurement, and service-level requirements.
- Client's desired degree of coordinator ownership.

Do not charge more simply because a client appears wealthy. Price the scope, service level, complexity, and risk.

## Discounts

### Discounts should never be offered when

- The project already has thin delivery margin.
- The prospect demands a guaranteed external outcome.
- The timeline is unusually urgent or operationally risky.
- Complexity or ownership is unclear.
- The discount would require reducing quality while preserving the same promise.
- The client is comparing Hajime only to a filing fee and does not value coordination.
- The client uses pressure, misrepresentation, or threatened reputation harm.
- A regulated, high-risk, or exception-heavy project requires extra oversight.

### Acceptable reasons for structured price adjustment

- A narrower package with genuinely reduced scope.
- A multi-project or enterprise volume commitment with forecastable demand.
- A partner or referral program with lower acquisition and onboarding cost.
- A pilot that produces explicit learning value, limited availability, and no misleading reference price.
- A nonprofit, public-interest, or founder-support initiative with a defined budget and selection policy.
- A prompt upgrade credit for a Blueprint whose work remains reusable.

Use scope changes, credits, or payment terms before discounting the same promise.

## Deposits and payment structure

Project packages should normally require an initial payment before onboarding and capacity reservation. Larger engagements may use milestones tied to planning approval, formation readiness, filing handoff, or operational launch.

Payment milestones are commercial, not claims that government or partner outcomes are guaranteed. The engagement terms define what happens if the client pauses, abandons, changes scope, or becomes ineligible.

Hajime should not front substantial third-party fees without cleared client funds and written authorization.

## Optional add-ons

Preferred add-ons are defined service outcomes:

- Additional stakeholder coordination.
- Additional entity or jurisdiction.
- Additional banking path.
- Expanded translation and certification coordination.
- Immigration professional coordination.
- Licensing or regulated-activity coordination.
- Complex address search.
- Remediation after rejection or material fact change.
- Post-launch stabilization extension.
- Ongoing concierge and annual compliance coordination.

Avoid “premium support” add-ons that merely restore responsiveness the base package should already provide.

## Recurring revenue opportunities

- Post-Launch Concierge retainers.
- Annual Compliance Coordination.
- Registered-address and mail-service coordination.
- Document-expiry and renewal coordination.
- Officer, ownership, and address change projects.
- Accountant, payroll, insurance, and provider review.
- Annual operating-readiness review.
- Repeat-country or repeat-entity launch programs.
- Enterprise reporting and partner-governance retainers.

Recurring services must deliver ongoing coordination value. Hajime should not create anxiety or unnecessary tasks to justify a subscription.

## Future enterprise offerings

Enterprise pricing may include:

- Program setup and country-readiness fees.
- Dedicated account or program capacity.
- Per-launch coordination fees.
- Volume commitments and reservation of partner capacity.
- Custom reporting and governance.
- Procurement, security, and contract requirements.
- Service-level commitments.
- Multi-country ongoing operations.

Enterprise discounts should reflect predictable volume or reduced marginal cost, not simply customer size.

## Margin discipline

Track economics by package, service, project type, coordinator effort, partner cost, exception load, and acquisition channel. A profitable average can hide an unprofitable customer segment or service promise.

Review:

- Coordinator active and waiting effort.
- Rework and escalation time.
- Partner-management cost.
- Third-party fee variance.
- Scope-change frequency.
- Time to completion.
- Ongoing-support attachment and retention.
- Client referral and acquisition cost.

When a package underperforms, change scope, qualification, process, or price. Do not solve poor economics by overloading coordinators.

## Pricing guardrails

1. Never imply a fee purchases approval from a bank, agency, or professional.
2. Separate Hajime coordination from professional and external costs.
3. Keep standard packages few and understandable.
4. Use written assumptions and scope-change triggers.
5. Preserve enough margin for exception handling and quality assurance.
6. Prefer reducing scope to discounting an unchanged promise.
7. Disclose partner economics where required and protect unbiased selection.
8. Do not use urgency, legal fear, or hidden fees to pressure a sale.
9. Review prices using observed effort and outcomes, not competitor imitation alone.
10. Premium pricing must produce a visibly premium operating experience.
