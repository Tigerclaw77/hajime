# Hajime Japan Customer Interview Guide

## Purpose

Discover how commercially committed founders currently plan and execute Japan entry, what creates enough pain to pay for help, and who controls the purchase. The interview is not a product demo.

Interview at least 20 prospective customers across the top three segments and 5 recent customers of competing approaches. Seek behavior and transactions, not opinions about a hypothetical service.

## Interview setup

- Duration: 30-45 minutes.
- Ask permission to take notes; do not record without explicit consent.
- State that Hajime is researching the process and is not providing legal, immigration, tax, accounting, financial, or other regulated advice.
- Ask for the most recent real example.
- Use neutral follow-ups: "What happened next?", "How did you decide that?", and "Can you give me an example?"
- Do not reveal packages or proposed pricing until after the core interview.

## Opening

> Thank you. I'm researching how international companies actually handle a Japan launch, especially the work between deciding to enter and becoming operational. I'm interested in what you did or are doing, including what did not work. There are no right answers, and I'm not here to advise on your legal or tax position.

## Context and trigger

1. Tell me about your company and your role.
2. When did Japan first become a serious business consideration?
3. What happened that moved it from an idea to an active project?
4. What date or event, if any, is the plan working toward?
5. What would happen commercially if the launch moved three months later?
6. Who inside the company cares most about the outcome?
7. What has already been approved: strategy, budget, customer commitment, hiring, or relocation?

## Current process

8. Walk me through everything you have done since the decision became active.
9. How did you decide what needed to happen first?
10. Which tasks are being handled internally?
11. Which external providers or public programs have you contacted?
12. How did you find and evaluate them?
13. Where do you currently track decisions, documents, owners, and dates?
14. When two providers need information from one another, who coordinates the handoff?
15. What is the next action today, and who owns it?
16. Which part of the process remains least clear?

## Pain and consequences

17. What has consumed more time than you expected?
18. Describe the most recent moment when you were unsure what happened next.
19. Where have you received conflicting or incomplete answers?
20. What information or document have you had to provide more than once?
21. What has been delayed, repeated, or abandoned?
22. Who inside the company absorbs the coordination work?
23. Roughly how many hours has that person spent so far?
24. What other work was displaced?
25. Which mistake or delay worries you most, and why?

Avoid asking "Would a single dashboard solve this?" That invites agreement without revealing whether the pain is valuable.

## Existing solutions and alternatives

26. What options did you consider before choosing the current approach?
27. Why did you reject the others?
28. What did you expect your current provider to handle?
29. What did you later discover was outside its scope?
30. Have you used JETRO, a city one-stop center, software, a law or scrivener office, an accountant, or a global formation provider? What happened?
31. What does your current approach do particularly well?
32. If you started again, what would you change?
33. Have you paid anyone specifically to coordinate across providers? Why or why not?

## Trust and boundaries

34. What makes you trust a Japan-entry provider?
35. Which credentials, references, or affiliations matter?
36. What would make you immediately distrust one?
37. When would you expect a licensed professional to take over?
38. Would you prefer one bundled provider or an independent coordinator working with several specialists? What drives that preference?
39. How should referral relationships or commissions be disclosed?
40. Which decisions would you never delegate?

## Decision-making and purchase

41. Who initiated the search for help?
42. Who evaluates providers?
43. Who can approve the spend?
44. Who can block the decision?
45. What documents or assurances are required before signing?
46. What other project would compete for the same budget?
47. How long did the last comparable service purchase take?
48. What caused it to move faster or slower?

## Budget and willingness to pay

Ask about actual spending before hypothetical prices.

49. What have you spent so far on professional help, government charges, software, office, translation, and internal time?
50. Which expense felt most worthwhile?
51. Which felt least worthwhile?
52. Was there a budget set before work began? Who set it?
53. What would have to be true for you to pay someone specifically to own planning and coordination?
54. Which tasks would you actually pay to remove from your team's workload?
55. Which tasks would you always handle yourself?
56. How would you compare a fixed project fee, monthly coordination fee, and hourly advice?

Only after these questions, show a neutral concept:

> Imagine a service that creates the launch plan, maps responsibilities and dependencies, consolidates requests, coordinates handoffs among your chosen specialists, tracks progress, and tells you the next action. Licensed professionals contract for and own their regulated work separately.

57. What, if anything, would this replace in your current process?
58. What is missing for it to be useful?
59. What part would you remove?
60. What evidence would you need before buying?
61. At what price would this be too inexpensive to trust?
62. At what price would it feel like a good value?
63. At what price would it become difficult to justify?
64. At what price would it be too expensive to consider?

Use these price-sensitivity questions as directional evidence, then make a real offer. Stated willingness is weaker than payment.

## Closing and paid test

65. Who else has gone through this recently and might describe a different experience?
66. May I contact you with a summary of what I learned?
67. Is there anything important about this process that I did not ask?

For qualified prospects only:

> We are offering a limited paid launch-readiness engagement. It includes structured intake, a dependency and responsibility map, preliminary cost and timeline ranges, and a review session. It costs [price] and begins [date]. Would you like to reserve one?

If they decline, ask:

68. What is the main reason you would not buy it now?
69. What event would need to occur before this became a priority?

Do not discount during the interview. Record the objection verbatim.

## Recent-customer variant

For someone who completed formation in the last 24 months, add:

- Which provider did you choose and what did the written scope include?
- What remained your responsibility after signing?
- Which milestone took longest?
- Where did a downstream provider reject or redo work?
- What did the whole process cost, including internal time?
- Would you hire the same provider again?
- What would you pay to avoid the hardest part?

## Synthesis rules

- Tag observations by segment and trigger, not just by interview.
- Count repeated behavior, but preserve outliers with high economic consequence.
- Separate "said useful," "requested proposal," and "paid."
- Do not merge regulated-advice demand with coordination demand.
- Revise the beachhead only after at least 10 interviews in a competing segment.
- Use exact customer language in sales copy only with permission or anonymization.
